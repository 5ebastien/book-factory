The mud sucked at her trainers. Cold air filled her lungs, smelling of smoke, wet wood, and pigs. The lane narrowed between houses of woven wattle. Men turned wooden cups on lathes at their doors. It was morning, raw and grey. York was gone. Grandad was gone. The dusk had vanished. She stood in the silt, the sunstone cold against her neck. A woman with a bucket stopped. She stared at Elsie’s jeans. "The town is the army’s now," the woman said. Elsie understood every word. "The Great Army took it last November," she added. "Over the sea. They are still here." The raven dropped onto Elsie’s shoulder. It felt like it had always been there. Its weight was solid, real. Muninn ruffled its feathers. "The frost melted three days ago," the raven said. "The lads fixed the roof with thatch." "The pigs ate the last turnip," Muninn continued. "The ice broke on the river." "Where are we going?" Elsie whispered. The woman frowned at the bird. "You have a strange pet," she said. "It looks hungry." "A cup-maker gave me a scrap of meat in this lane once," Muninn said. The woman sighed heavily. "Wait by the house. The army patrols the lanes. It is safer here." "I flew down this river once," Muninn added. "The water sang in the stones." "Stay put," the woman insisted. "Do not wander." Elsie gripped the raven’s legs. The mud was deep and cold. The smell of the pigs was overwhelming. The sunstone pulsed gently. The lane stretched out before her. The woman turned away, bucket in hand. Muninn shifted on her shoulder. The woman’s back was to her now. The raven’s eyes were black and deep. Elsie looked at the mud.
