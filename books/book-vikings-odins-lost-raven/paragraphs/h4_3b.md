By the second day, the fog had swallowed the horizon. The sea and the sky were no longer separate things. They were one grey, wet mass that pressed against the hull of the longship. Spray stung Elsie’s face, cold and sharp as needles. She wore her padded coat, zipped to the chin, her trainers slipping on the wet deck. Ketil huddled near the mast, shivering, his eyes fixed on the white nothingness ahead. He looked small against the wooden planks.

Nobody could see the sun. Without it, they were blind. The ship drifted in a slow, uneasy circle. Grim sat at the steering oar, his knuckles white, saying nothing. The old men murmured in low voices, their words lost in the wind. Elie reached into her coat and pulled out the sunstone. It was warm, heavy, and round in her palm. She lifted it up, above the railing, into the thick air.

A single bright point glowed through the fog. It was steady and blue-white. Elsie pointed her finger at the spot. The light seemed to anchor itself there, refusing to move. Grim looked at her. Then he looked at the bright point. A rough laugh rumbled in his chest. He spun the steering oar hard to the left. The ship groaned as it turned.

"That is where the sun is," he said. "You see it."
Elsie nodded, her heart hammering against her ribs. She did not let go of the stone.

Grim called out to the crew, his voice cutting through the wind. They adjusted their course, following the line she had drawn in the air. He said the old men talked about stones like this. He said he had never believed them. Now he watched the bright point and smiled.

The fog began to thin, just slightly. The white edge sharpened into grey. And there, emerging from the mist, were mountains. They stood tall and black against the sky. The sea rushed up against their dark sides.
