The island had no trees on it at all. It was a hump of black rock with grass on top, sticking up out of the grey sea, and it was covered in birds: gulls and puffins and things Elsie had no names for, every one of them screaming. The smell was unbelievable. There was a stream, though, and the crew filled the barrels one by one and shouted at the birds, which shouted back. Bard did not help; he walked Elsie along the side of the Sea-Hart where she lay pulled up on the shingle.

All along the rail the crew's shields hung in a row, overlapping, round and painted yellow and black, so that the ship looked as if she had scales. Elsie had noticed them on the first day and had not asked. The one at the very end was smaller than the rest. Its paint was older, and there was a split in the rim that somebody had mended with a strip of iron.

"That was mine," said Bard. "When I was Ketil's size. My uncle gave it me the day I first crewed on her." He rubbed the mended split with his thumb. "Everybody who crews the Sea-Hart has a shield on her rail. That's the rule."

He lifted it off its peg and held it out. It was lighter than Elsie expected, lime wood with a leather strap on the back, and it covered her from her chin to her knees.

Behind them, out to sea, the grey had begun to go white. Fog was coming in over the water in a low wall, and the far end of the island had already gone, as quietly as a lamp put out. Bard looked at it and groaned, because he did not want to lose the wind. Ketil sat on the rocks with the birds screaming round his head, and he looked as though he would happily wait there for a week.
