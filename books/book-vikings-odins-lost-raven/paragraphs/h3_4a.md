The oar bit into the water with a heavy, dull thud. It was heavier than any tree branch she had ever used. Elsie pulled, and the Sea-Hart answered with a slow, rolling swing. Her palms began to sting before the first mile was behind them. She sat on the cold wood of the sea chest, feeling every rock under the hull. There were no benches, so the deck was for standing or sitting on cargo. Ketil sat opposite her, his knees drawn up to his chest. He looked small against the dark timber. Bard stood near the stern, watching the water pass. He did not sing. He only watched. The other rowers found a rhythm. They sang a low, droning tune about a woman and a troll. The song was monotonous and strangely catchy. Elsie’s hands started to burn raw. She tried to change her grip. The wood slipped in her fingers. A few drops of sweat fell onto the deck. The river Ouse wound through flat, wet country. There was no one on the banks but the reeds. The air smelled of mud and low-lying fog. It grew heavier as the afternoon wore on. The current slowed, then seemed to stop. The town’s smoke was a faint grey smudge behind them. Ahead, the water widened into a great brown mouth. The Humber opened up, vast and indifferent. Past that lay the sea, waiting in the distance. Elsie pulled the oar through the water. The pull was steady and unbroken.
