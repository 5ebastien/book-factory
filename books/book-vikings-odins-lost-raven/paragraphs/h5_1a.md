The ship lay on the grass, and it was the most beautiful thing Elsie had ever seen. It was long and low, built for a fjord rather than a field. The prow curled up into a tight spiral of polished wood. Every inch of the hull was carved with twisting animals. They looked like they were moving, even though the ship was still. On the deck stood a grave chamber made of timber. It was a little house, clean and bright in the pale light. Around the ship, forty people pulled on ropes. They hauled the great vessel up from the shore on rollers. The noise was a low, grinding rumble that shook the ground. On the grass beside the ship waited the lady’s things. Beds and chests stood in neat rows. There were sledges and a carved wagon, ready to go in with her. The lady had died in the winter, and in three days she would be buried in this ship with everything she needed. Muninn sat on top of the spiral prow. The raven did not come down. He looked at the workmen, then at Elsie. His eyes were hard and dark, like chips of obsidian. Elsie felt the cold bite at her fingers, but she did not move. The air smelled of damp earth and sharp pine. The sun was low, casting long shadows across the trench on the hill. The ropes creaked as the workers hauled another heavy roll forward. The ship groaned in response, a deep wooden sound. Muninn tilted his head, watching the sledges. Elsie stared at the carved animals on the hull. One of them had a mouth full of teeth. She stepped back, her boot slipping on the frozen grass. The crowd moved with a steady, mechanical rhythm. The ship rose, inch by inch, toward the mound.
