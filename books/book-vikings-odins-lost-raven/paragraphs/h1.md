On Monday afternoon Grandad drove them into York with the stone in a sandwich bag in his inside pocket. The raven followed the car. Elsie watched it in the wing mirror all the way, flapping from lamp post to lamp post, never more than one behind.

The finds officer at the Yorkshire Museum could see them at two. They were early, so they sat on a bench in Museum Gardens with their breath smoking. The museum's big stone pillars stood in front of them. Behind them were the broken walls of the old abbey, and the raven landed on the highest one and folded its wings.

Then there was a man at the other end of the bench.

Nobody had seen him sit down. He had a long coat and a wide hat, and one eye. He was looking at Grandad's inside pocket as though he could see through the coat.

"Afternoon," said Grandad, in the voice he had once used on rowdy passengers on the late bus.

"Found anything?" said the man.

Grandad sat up straight. "We have. And it's going in there today, to be written down and reported, because that's the law. And because if it's in a case in there, everybody gets to see it. Not just us."

The man smiled. "Some things are lent," he said, "not found."

Elsie took the bag out of Grandad's pocket before he could stop her. She held the stone to her eye and looked up at the raven on the wall.

It remembered something. She heard it plainly over the traffic: a long road going north, over water and then up, and a table at the end of it with an empty place. The road home did not start in a museum.

Then the raven lifted off the wall and flew towards the gate. It looked back at her, and waited.
