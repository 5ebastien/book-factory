Elsie Thorpe was thirteen, and she had been bored by more museums than anybody else in York; her grandad said this was because she had been taken to more museums than anybody else in York, and he was probably right. Every Saturday of the year, whatever the weather, Grandad went metal-detecting. He had driven buses for forty years, and now he walked fields instead, very slowly, sweeping the ground in front of him while he listened to the beeps in his headphones. Elsie went with him because her mum worked Saturdays. She told her friends it was because she had no choice, although she never told them that she liked it.

This Saturday was the first one in January, and the field outside York was ploughed and frozen hard, with frost lying white in every furrow. The sky was one flat grey lid; the sun was somewhere behind it, not trying. At the bottom of the field, by the hedge, the detector gave its double note. Grandad's knees were bad in the cold, so Elsie knelt and dug while he stood over her with his breath smoking. Six inches down, the trowel scraped something that was not a ring-pull.

It was a clear crystal the size of her thumb, set in a mount of blackened silver on a thong of black leather gone stiff with cold. She rubbed the mud off and held it up to the sky. Through it, in the middle of all that grey, there was one bright point, exactly where the sun had to be.

"Found anything?"

A man was leaning on the gate into the lane, although nobody had heard him come. He was old, in a long coat and a wide hat, and he had only one eye; the other was shut in a fold of skin.

"Only rubbish," said Grandad.

When Elsie looked again, the lane was empty all the way to the bend.
