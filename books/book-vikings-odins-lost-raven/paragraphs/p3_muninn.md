The raven was still there on Sunday morning. There was frost on the window and frost on the raven, and although the yard below had filled with light, it had not moved at all. Elsie sat up in bed and looked at it; it looked back. Then, because she could not think of anything else to do, she held the stone up to her eye and looked at it through that.

The raven spoke.

It was not a squawk or a croak but a voice, dry and creaking, like a gate hinge that had learned words. It said that on a morning long ago it had flown from its master's shoulder at first light, and that it had not come back. That was eleven hundred winters ago. It said nothing else; it only remembered, out loud, and then it was quiet.

Elsie went downstairs very fast. Grandad was making toast, and she told him, and she waited for him to laugh. He did not. He put the butter knife down and sat, while the grill ticked behind him in the quiet.

"Odin had two ravens," he said. "Every detectorist in Yorkshire knows that much. Huginn and Muninn, Thought and Memory. He sent them out every morning to fly over the world, and at night they came back and told him everything." He rubbed his chin. "There's an old poem where he says he's afraid Thought won't come home. But he's more afraid for Memory."

"Which one's on my window?"

"If it's talking about the past," said Grandad, "I'd say that's Muninn."

He was quiet for a while. Then he told her the other thing, about the wolf: Sköll ran after the sun across the sky every day, and at the end of the world he would catch it. Elsie looked down at the stone in her hand. Even here, under the kitchen light, the bright point was there, small and steady, as if it were waiting. She did not finish her toast.
