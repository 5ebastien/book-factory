Grandad's kitchen smelled of toast and the paraffin heater, and the window over the sink had gone black while they ate. After tea he looked the stone up on his laptop, with his glasses pushed up on his forehead where they were no use to anybody, and read bits out loud.

"Sunstone," he said. "The old sagas talk about one. A stone you could hold up to a cloudy sky to find the sun." He scrolled. "Nobody's ever found one. Not at a real Viking site. There's a crystal off an old shipwreck, but that's hundreds of years too late."

"So this is the first one."

"So this is the first strange thing about it."

He shut the laptop and took his glasses off his forehead and put them on the table, which meant he was being serious. Then he told her the law. A find like this might be treasure, and treasure had to be reported to the coroner within fourteen days; on Monday they would take it to the finds officer at the Yorkshire Museum, who would tell the coroner, and after that it was out of their hands.

"It's not ours, then," said Elsie.

"It was never ours, love. We just found it."

She went up to bed with it round her neck anyway, under her pyjamas, where it sat cold against her skin until slowly it grew warm. The back bedroom at Grandad's had a window over the yard, and when she went to draw the curtains there was a raven on the sill. It was big, much bigger than a crow, and black with a grey sheen like oil on a puddle. It looked at her while she banged on the glass. It did not go away.
