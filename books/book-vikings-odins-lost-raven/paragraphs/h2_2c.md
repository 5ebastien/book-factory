The wool sank into the basket, and Elsie watched the threads tangle softly. The Sea-Hart lay low in the water, its oak planks overlapping like the scales of a large, quiet fish. Bard stood in the stern, broad and weathered, his eyes fixed on the neck where the stone hung. He did not smile, and the air between them felt thick with the smell of tar and old wood.

He asked for the stone, and Elsie passed it over with a trembling hand. Bard held it up against the pale sky, turning it slowly until a bright point caught the cloud. He whistled, a short, sharp sound that cut through the low hum of the dock.

"Men say such stones find the sun in fog," he said, his voice rough with age. "But no man I have met ever owned one." He looked at Elsie, and his eyes were hard as flint. "What do you want for it?"

She said it was not hers to sell. The words came out flat, and she heard herself sound like a stranger. Bard did not argue, and he did not look angry, only thoughtful. He lowered the stone and turned back to the rigging, where the rope creaked gently in the wind. The ship waited, patient and still, while the morning stretched out before them.
