The steps went down further than the hall was high, into a warm dark that smelled of hay and horse. Over Elsie's head the noise of Valhalla went on, muffled: shouting, and the scrape of benches, and under it all the click of Sköll's claws on the floor. At the bottom there was a door. It was old and black and bound with iron, and it had no handle and no keyhole; when Elsie pushed it, it did not move.

Muninn remembered, in her ear, a door that opened only to things made to keep danger off. So Elsie held up what she had been given on the crossing, the thing that had been meant to keep danger off her all the way to Norway, and she felt stupid while she held it up in the light of the stone. The door swung back without a sound, as if it knew.

Inside was a stable with one stall, and in the stall stood a horse. He was grey, the grey of a winter sky, and very tall, and he had eight legs. Elsie counted them twice. They stood under him in pairs, neatly, like a table with too many legs that had never once fallen over.

He had been standing there a long time, and there was dust on his back. Very quietly, Muninn remembered the last morning it had seen him: its master swinging up onto him at dawn, and the raven flying on ahead and not coming back. Since then nobody had ridden him anywhere. His name was Sleipnir.

He put his long nose down to Muninn on Elsie's shoulder and blew through his nostrils very softly, the way a horse greets another horse it knows, while Muninn pressed its head against his face. Then Sleipnir knelt, all eight knees, one pair after another, until his back was low enough for Elsie to climb on.
