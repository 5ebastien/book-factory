Sköll opened his jaws for the stone.

Elsie had two things to meet him with, and a great deal of luck. The first thing did its job; the second did half of it, and the luck did the rest. The wolf's teeth closed a finger's width from her hand, and then he was backing away down the hall and out of the west door with nothing, after the sun, where he belonged.

Muninn went up her arm and onto the one-eyed man's shoulder, and remembered a frozen field, and was home.

The one-eyed man took off his wide hat, and Elsie knew him. Odin.

"Nothing worth having is free," he said. "The road home is open. But it goes the long way round."

The long way went back through Jorvik. When the light cleared, Elsie was standing on the muddy riverbank by the long ships, in spring, while the road behind her shut; it did not open again for a whole summer.

She lived that summer. She mended nets and carried water, and she learned to row badly and to count in Norse well. A stonecutter by the old Roman gate let her cut sixteen runes into a stone nobody wanted, and she spelled her name with them, crooked. On the last night of the summer the road opened again, and she stepped through it onto Ouse Bridge at dusk, on the same Monday she had left.

Grandad was there. He looked at her for a long time.

"You look older," he said.

She did not tell him why. In the Yorkshire Museum, in the Viking gallery, there was a runestone with a girl's name on it in sixteen runes, crooked. Nobody could have cut it but her.
