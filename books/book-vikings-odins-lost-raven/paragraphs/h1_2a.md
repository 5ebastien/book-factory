By half past three the light was going, the way it did in January, all at once. The lamps on Ouse Bridge came on one after another, and underneath them the river ran brown and high and fast; it carried a whole branch past with a crow still sitting on it. Grandad leaned on the parapet next to Elsie and got his breath back. For a while he did not say anything. He only looked at her with his head on one side, the way he looked at a signal he could not place.

"You know what your gran used to say about me?" he said. "That she never knew where I was. I'd go out after one more signal and come back in the dark with my pockets full of bottle tops." He sniffed. "She bought me this so she could see me coming across the fields."

He pulled the head-torch off over his woolly hat. It was the one he wore for detecting on winter evenings, a fat lamp on a stretchy band, and it had left a red line across his forehead.

"If there's going to be ravens and dark and you all at once," he said, "I want to be able to see where you are."

He put it on her, and it slid down over one eyebrow and smelled of his hat. When he clicked it on, a white beam went out over the water and lit the railings of King's Staith below. The raven was sitting on them. It had been waiting there the whole time, and although the light fell full on it, it did not even blink.

"Or," said Grandad, "there's a café on Coppergate that does a very good hot chocolate. That bird has waited eleven hundred years. It can wait twenty minutes."

Elsie looked at the raven, while the raven looked at the river.
