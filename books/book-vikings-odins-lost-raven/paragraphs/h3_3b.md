The shipyard was the only quiet place left in Jorvik. The fighting was a noise over the roofs at the far end of the town; here there was only the river slapping at the slipway, and the smell of tar, and the Sea-Hart. She was still up on her rollers, long and dark and ready, with her oars stacked inside her like a bundle of sticks. Nobody was near her at all except an old man with a white beard, who sat on an oak log with his hands on his knees. He was the shipwright, and he had stayed, he said, because a ship was a ship and somebody had to.

"She won't go," said Ketil. He was pushing at the stern until his face went red.

The old man pointed with his chin. One of the rollers under the keel had slewed sideways and jammed hard against a wooden stake driven into the mud. From a basket of tools he fetched an iron wedge as long as Elsie's hand, dark and blunt, and a wooden mallet, and he put them both into her hands.

"Ships aren't sawn out of trees," he said. "They're split. Along the grain, with one of these. Oak wants to open, if you ask it right." He showed her the crack in the top of the stake. "There. Then hit it like you mean it."

Elsie set the wedge in the crack and hit it. She hit it again, harder, while Muninn shouted memories in her ear, and on the fourth blow the stake split down its whole length with a crack like a shot. The roller spun free. The Sea-Hart groaned and slid until her stern went into the river with a slap. Ketil was already scrambling aboard for the sail ropes, and Muninn flew up onto the prow to look downriver. Along the strand, Bard and his crew were coming at a run.
