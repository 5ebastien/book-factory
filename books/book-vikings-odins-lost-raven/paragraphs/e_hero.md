Sköll opened his jaws for the stone, and Elsie did not let go of it.

Everything she had carried did its job, one after another, as if each thing had been waiting for this. Grandad's head-torch threw its white beam straight into the wolf's eyes, while the shipwright's wedge went between his teeth and held them open. What the Sea-Hart had given her kept the heat of him off; what the Tree had given her made every warrior in the hall stand up at once, on her side. Sleipnir, who had carried her up out of the dark, stamped all eight feet on the floor, and the whole of Valhalla shook.

Sköll backed away. He backed all the way down the hall and out of the west door with nothing, and went back to chasing the sun, where he belonged. Then it was very quiet. Muninn walked up Elsie's arm and hopped across onto the one-eyed man's shoulder, and settled there as if it had never been gone. It remembered, out loud, a morning over a frozen field; then it remembered this one.

The one-eyed man took off his wide hat, and Elsie saw who he was. Odin. Of course it was.

"Do I have to pay?" she asked.

"You paid on the way here," said Odin. "Every step of it. Go home."

She was standing outside the Yorkshire Museum at dusk, on the same Monday, while Grandad hurried towards her across the grass and called her name, not cross at all. Two weeks later the stone went into a glass case in the Viking gallery, with a label that had Grandad's name on it, and hers. On the museum roof a raven sat in the cold and looked down at the people going in. It seemed to be remembering something.
