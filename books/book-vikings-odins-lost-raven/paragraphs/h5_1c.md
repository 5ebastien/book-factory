The air inside the hall smelled of hot tallow and damp wool, a heavy scent that coated the back of Elsie’s throat. She stood near the entrance, her trainers squeaking faintly on the wooden floorboards, while the raven on her shoulder preened a black feather with slow, deliberate care.

The room was crowded with men and women carrying buckets and crates, their movements quick and urgent. Barrels of ale rolled across the stone floor, and a whole ox lay gutted and bleeding on a trestle table, its skin pale in the dim light. Stacks of bread were piled high against the wall, loose and fragile, looking like dry firewood waiting for a spark.

A tall woman with a ring of heavy iron keys at her belt moved through the crowd, shouting orders that were lost in the noise of the chopping and the splashing. She had no tears in her eyes, only a tight, hard line of control that made the air around her feel colder. When she stopped and looked directly at the black shape on Elsie’s shoulder, the noise in the hall seemed to drain away.

She pointed a finger at the door and sent the workers out, one by one, until only Elsie, the raven, and the woman remained. The sound of the last worker’s feet faded down the stone steps, leaving a silence that felt thick and wet.

The woman stepped closer, her eyes fixed on Muninn, not on Elsie. "My mother saw you," she said, her voice low and flat, like a stone dropped into deep water. "In a dream, the night before she died. You were walking away from this hall, going home."

Muninn tilted its head, the feathers on its neck ruffling slightly, and let out a soft, croaking sound that carried the weight of a winter snowfall long melted. The woman watched the raven blink, her face unchanged, while the smell of the ox grew stronger in the still air.
