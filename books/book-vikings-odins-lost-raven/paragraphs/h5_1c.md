The hall smelled of wet wool and raw meat. Barrels of ale lined the stone floor, capped with green cloth. In the center, a whole ox hung from a beam, its hide dark and glossy. Stacks of round bread leaned against the walls like chopped firewood. The air was thick with the sound of chopping knives and humming voices. Elsie stood near the door, feeling small. Muninn shifted on her shoulder, his feathers ruffled against the draft. A tall woman stopped moving in the middle of the room. She wore a heavy woolen dress and a long ring of iron keys at her belt. The keys clinked softly as she turned her head. Her eyes fixed on the raven. She did not blink. The hall went quiet, one person at a time. The tall woman waved a hand, and the cooks drifted out into the fog. Laughter died on lips. Doors slammed shut in the distance. Now only Elsie and the woman remained. The smell of the ox grew stronger. "My mother saw a bird like that," the woman said. Her voice was flat and dry. It was the night before she died. "It was flying toward home." Muninn tilted his head, a single black eye reflecting the firelight. "He needs to get back," Elsie said. Her voice shook, but she kept her hand on the raven’s wing. It was not hers to let go. It was hers to take home. The woman stared at the raven, then at Elsie. Her jaw tightened. "We bury her in three days," the woman said. "With her ship." She looked at the door, then back at Elsie. "Let the bird fly before the boat leaves. It belongs to the sky." "He is going home," Elsie said. "I am taking him there." The raven rumbled on her shoulder, a deep, low sound of warning. The woman stepped closer, her keys jangling like wind chimes in a storm.
