Elsie came down onto the trampled mud of the riverbank, her trainers squelching in the grey sludge. A dozen long ships lay drawn up in the brown water, their hulls dark and heavy against the ice. When she lowered the sunstone from her neck, the ships stayed put. It was morning, a raw grey spring morning that smelled of tar, smoke, and rotting fish. Men were rolling heavy barrels up a wooden plank, their breath puffing in white clouds. One of them looked at her and said the town belonged to the army now. He told her she had better keep out of its way. Elsie understood every word, though she had never learned this dialect in her life. The raven dropped onto her shoulder with a thud, as if he had always sat there. He shifted his weight, his black feathers sleek and wet. Muninn did not speak, but he held the memory of the wheat fields burning in November. He remembered the sound of the bells before the silence took them. Elsie looked down at the mud, her jeans soaked and heavy with the cold. The sunstone rested against her collarbone, warm and solid in her hand. She could feel the pulse of it under her skin, steady and quiet. The men continued their work, ignoring her presence in the freezing air. The smell of tar was thick, sticking to the back of her throat. Smoke curled from the roofs of the houses behind the ships, rising into the pale sky. The frost was melting into the earth, turning the ground into a thick soup. Elsie looked toward the city walls, which stood tall and still against the grey. She did not move, her hands staying deep in her sleeves. The barrels clattered against the planks, a dull, rhythmic sound. The water lapped at the hull of the nearest ship, cold and endless. She breathed in the air, tasting salt and iron. The stone hummed quietly against her chest. The raven watched the men with one bright eye. Elsie stood still, watching the mud swirl around her feet.
